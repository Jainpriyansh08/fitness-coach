## 1. When to research

- Every medical condition or special-population factor **this client** has (not a generic list).
- Every home remedy, supplement or "trend" you suggest, or that the client/coach asks about.
- Any claim the coach wants to tell a client as fact.
- Anything where `conditions-primer.md` or `remedies-supplements.md` may be out of date
  (guidelines are updated; supplements get new safety warnings; trials get retracted).

## 2. How to research

Use web search when available. Prefer, in order:

1. **Clinical guidelines and position stands:** ACSM, WHO, ADA Standards of Care, ACOG, American
   Thyroid Association, Endocrine Society, International PCOS guideline, NICE, ISSN position
   stands, AHA/ESC (blood pressure), OARSI/ACR (osteoarthritis).
2. **Indian guidance:** ICMR-NIN _Dietary Guidelines for Indians_ (2024) and _Indian Food
   Composition Tables_ (IFCT 2017), RSSDI (diabetes), FOGSI (obstetrics/gynaecology), FSSAI
   (supplement regulation), NFHS-5 (population data).
3. **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** (Cochrane, BJSM, Sports Medicine, AJCN, etc.).
4. **High-quality RCTs and large cohort studies.**
5. **Safety databases:** NIH LiverTox (herb/supplement liver injury), NIH Office of Dietary
   Supplements fact sheets, regulator safety notices.

Avoid as evidence: brand sites, influencer content, press releases without the paper, single
small studies presented as proof, animal/cell studies presented as human effects.

Check: is the trial retracted or corrected? Is there a newer guideline? Is the population
similar (age, sex, diabetes status, Indian/South Asian)? Is the dose realistic in food form?

## 3. Evidence grades (use these exact words)

| Grade                       | Meaning                                                                                                           |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Strong**                  | Consistent findings across several good-quality RCTs or meta-analyses, or a firm guideline recommendation         |
| **Moderate**                | Supported by human trials/meta-analyses but effects are modest, results vary, or quality is mixed                 |
| **Limited**                 | Small/short human studies, inconsistent results, or mostly animal/lab data                                        |
| **Traditional**             | Long-standing cultural practice with little direct research — may be fine to use if safe, but don't claim effects |
| **Expert consensus**        | Professional agreement or guideline practice points without strong trial data                                     |
| **Not supported / against** | Evidence shows no benefit or a risk that outweighs benefit                                                        |

Always pair the grade with the size of the effect in plain words ("lowers after-meal sugar a
little", "about 1–2 kg extra lean mass over months") — a strong grade for a tiny effect is still
a tiny effect.

## 4. Citation rules

- Cite real sources only: organisation or first author, title (short), journal/publisher, **year**,
  and a **link** (DOI or official page) you actually saw. Put full details in the plan's
  `evidence` list (→ Evidence & Sources tab).
- If you can't access the web: say "Web research isn't available in this session — the points
  below are from training knowledge and should be checked before use", and give citations only
  where you are confident they exist, marked "unverified".
- Never fabricate a DOI, PMID, page number, statistic or quote. If unsure of a detail, leave it out.
- Separate **what the source says** from **your interpretation** for this client.
- Client-facing text gets the evidence _label_, not the citation list.

## 5. Condition research template (Stage 1, section 2)

For each condition the client actually has:

```
### <Condition> — <client's status: e.g. diagnosed 2021, on levothyroxine, last reviewed ? [Client-stated]>
1. What it is & why it matters here — 2–3 sentences, plain.
2. What the coach still needs to know — questions (call vs clinician).
3. Effect on fatigue, recovery, activity tolerance, appetite/diet.
4. Training considerations — evidence-supported (grade), and what to avoid or modify.
5. Nutrition considerations — evidence-supported (grade); what NOT to restrict without reason.
6. Warning signs & referral criteria — specific symptoms → which professional, how urgently.
7. Common fitness myths to avoid — and the accurate version to tell the client.
8. Coach can manage vs. needs clinical supervision.
Sources: 2–5 citations (guideline first).
```

## 6. Remedy / supplement card template

```
**<Name>** — <Home remedy / Food / Supplement>
Claim people make: …
Evidence: <grade> — <what the research actually shows, effect size in plain words, dose used>
Fit for this client: <why it may or may not help THEM, given goals/conditions/meds/diet>
How to use (if used): <amount, timing, preparation, duration, how we'll judge if it helps>
Cautions: <who should avoid; medication interactions; pregnancy/breastfeeding; allergies; side-effects>
Alternatives: <food-first or behaviour options, usually with stronger evidence>
Recommendation: <include optional / include after doctor check / don't include> → Decision Brief item
Sources: …
```

In the workbook (Supplements & Remedies tab) each item needs: `name`, `type`, `why_for_you`,
`evidence`, `how`, `cautions`, `alternative`, `status` — the builder refuses items without an
evidence grade or cautions.

## 7. Conflicting evidence and opinions

When good sources or experienced practitioners disagree (e.g. medical clearance for sedentary
people with diabetes; carb timing; gluten-free for Hashimoto's; cycle-based training; daily
weighing; "avoid starchy vegetables"):

1. Summarise each position in one line with its best source and grade.
2. Say what it would mean for _this_ client and the risk if each choice is wrong.
3. Give your recommendation — then put it in the Decision Brief as **Your call**.
4. Once the coach decides, log it in `decisions` (Decision Log) with the date.

## 8. Seed sources (verified to exist as of late 2026 — still check for newer versions)

**Screening & activity guidelines**

- Riebe D et al. Updating ACSM's recommendations for exercise preparticipation health screening. _Med Sci Sports Exerc_ 2015;47(11):2473–79. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000000664
- ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription (latest edition).
- Bull FC et al. WHO 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour. _Br J Sports Med_ 2020;54:1451–62. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955 · WHO page: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128
- Ding D et al. Daily steps and health outcomes in adults: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis. _Lancet Public Health_ 2025;10(8):e668–81 (≈7,000 steps/day vs 2,000: ~47% lower all-cause mortality; small extra gain to 10,000).

**Diabetes / metabolic**

- Kanaley JA et al. Exercise/physical activity in individuals with type 2 diabetes: ACSM consensus statement. _Med Sci Sports Exerc_ 2022;54(2):353–68. doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000002800
- Colberg SR et al. Physical activity/exercise and diabetes: ADA position statement. _Diabetes Care_ 2016;39(11):2065–79. doi:10.2337/dc16-1728
- ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes — current year's edition (diabetesjournals.org/care).
- Buffey AJ et al. Interrupting prolonged sitting with standing or light-intensity walking. _Sports Med_ 2022;52(8):1765–87. doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01649-4 (light walking breaks lowered post-meal glucose and insulin vs sitting; walking > standing).

**Blood pressure**

- Edwards JJ et al. Exercise training and resting blood pressure: pairwise and network meta-analysis of 270 RCTs. _Br J Sports Med_ 2023. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2022-106503 (isometric training — e.g. wall squats — gave the largest reductions; aerobic, dynamic resistance and combined training also effective).

**Thyroid, PCOS, pregnancy**

- Jonklaas J et al. Guidelines for the treatment of hypothyroidism (ATA). _Thyroid_ 2014;24(12):1670–751.
- Ross DS et al. 2016 ATA guidelines for diagnosis and management of hyperthyroidism. _Thyroid_ 2016;26(10):1343–421.
- Teede HJ et al. 2023 International evidence-based guideline for the assessment and management of PCOS (Monash University; published in _Hum Reprod_, _JCEM_, _Fertil Steril_, _Eur J Endocrinol_ 2023).
- ACOG Committee Opinion 804: Physical activity and exercise during pregnancy and the postpartum period. _Obstet Gynecol_ 2020;135:e178–88.

**Sports nutrition**

- Jäger R et al. ISSN position stand: protein and exercise. _J Int Soc Sports Nutr_ 2017;14:20. doi:10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8 (1.4–2.0 g/kg/day for most exercising people; ~0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g per meal; 2.3–3.1 g/kg for resistance-trained people in a deficit).
- Morton RW et al. Protein supplementation and resistance-training gains: meta-analysis. _Br J Sports Med_ 2018;52:376–84. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608 (benefit plateaus ~1.6 g/kg/day).
- Kreider RB et al. ISSN position stand: creatine supplementation. _J Int Soc Sports Nutr_ 2017;14:18.
- Guest NS et al. ISSN position stand: caffeine and exercise performance. _J Int Soc Sports Nutr_ 2021;18:1.
- Mifflin MD et al. A new predictive equation for resting energy expenditure. _Am J Clin Nutr_ 1990;51:241–47.
- ICMR-NIN. Dietary Guidelines for Indians, 2024. · Longvah T et al. Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT), NIN 2017.

**Remedies & supplements**

- Neelakantan N et al. Effect of fenugreek intake on glycemia: meta-analysis of clinical trials. _Nutr J_ 2014;13:7. doi:10.1186/1475-2891-13-7 (lower fasting/2-h glucose and HbA1c, mainly in people with diabetes at ≥ medium doses; most trials low quality).
- Shishehbor F et al. Vinegar consumption can attenuate postprandial glucose and insulin responses: systematic review and meta-analysis. _Diabetes Res Clin Pract_ 2017;127:1–9 (PMID 28292654).
- **Retracted — do not cite:** the 2024 _BMJ Nutrition, Prevention & Health_ apple-cider-vinegar weight-loss trial in Lebanese adolescents/young adults was retracted in September 2025 after independent statisticians could not reproduce its results.
- Gencer B et al. Long-term marine omega-3 supplementation and atrial fibrillation risk: meta-analysis of RCTs. _Circulation_ 2021 (HR ≈1.25 overall; higher risk at > 1 g/day).
- Gardiner C et al. The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: systematic review and meta-analysis. _Sleep Med Rev_ 2023;69:101764 (~45 min less sleep; coffee ≥ 8.8 h and a pre-workout dose ≥ 13.2 h before bed to avoid losing sleep time).
- NIH LiverTox — entries for ashwagandha, turmeric/curcumin, green tea extract, Garcinia and other herbals (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547852/).
- NIH Office of Dietary Supplements fact sheets (ods.od.nih.gov) — vitamin D, B12, iron, magnesium, omega-3.
